## A Sunday Visit in Berlin

On Sunday 7 October 1900, Heinrich Rubens and his wife came to dinner at Max Planck's house in Berlin, and Rubens told his host that Wilhelm Wien's radiation law was wrong. Not wrong everywhere — in the far infrared. Wien's 1896 formula predicted that the energy radiated by a hot cavity should level off at long wavelengths, becoming insensitive to temperature. The measurements Rubens had been making with Ferdinand Kurlbaum, out at wavelengths as long as 51 micrometres, showed it climbing in direct proportion to the absolute temperature instead. Planck went to work once his guests had left and posted Rubens the resulting formula on a postcard that same night.

Twelve days later, on 19 October, Planck read it out to the German Physical Society — as an extended remark in the discussion following Kurlbaum's paper on the new measurements — a formula that fitted both ends of the spectrum: an energy density going as ν³ divided by exp(bν/T) − 1. It was an interpolation, with no derivation behind it at all.

Getting one cost him two months and his classical convictions. Planck reached for Boltzmann's statistical entropy, S = k log W — a method he had spent years resisting on principle. Counting the arrangements W of energy among his cavity resonators required that energy come in finite lumps, and consistency with Wien's displacement law required the lump to scale with frequency: ε = hν. On 14 December 1900 he presented the derivation, along with a new constant of nature, h ≈ 6.55 × 10⁻²⁷ erg·s. The same fit yielded Boltzmann's constant, k ≈ 1.34 × 10⁻¹⁶ erg/K.

Planck did not think he had discovered that energy is granular. Writing to Robert Wood in 1931, he called the move "a purely formal assumption and I really did not give it much thought except that no matter what the cost, I must bring about a positive result." He published nothing further on black-body radiation between 1901 and 1906, and his 1906 lectures on thermal radiation quietly presented a continuum theory. Only around 1908, pressed by Hendrik Lorentz, did he concede that the quantum of action was irreducible.

One piece of the standard telling deserves dismantling here. The "ultraviolet catastrophe" — the classical prediction that a cavity should radiate infinite energy at short wavelengths — did not drive Planck. The phrase was coined years after 1900, and historians have argued that attaching it to his motivation is a retrofit. His problem was a discrepancy in the infrared, not a divergence in the ultraviolet.

## The Heuristic Nobody Wanted

Planck had quantized the walls. In March 1905, Einstein quantized the light. His paper in *Annalen der Physik* carried the word "heuristic" in its title, and the hedge was deliberate: he was proposing that "the energy of light is distributed discontinuously in space… it consists of a finite number of energy quanta."

The argument was thermodynamic. Einstein computed how the entropy of radiation in the high-frequency regime varied with the volume of its container, and found it varied exactly as the entropy of an ideal gas of independent particles does. Radiation behaved statistically like a gas of separate energy packets, each of size hν.

Then he cashed the idea out. Heinrich Hertz had noticed in 1887 that ultraviolet light made sparks jump more readily; Philipp Lenard established the awkward details. Brighter light ejected more electrons from a metal, but not faster ones. Below a threshold frequency, nothing happened however intense the beam. Emission began essentially instantly. Every one of those facts contradicted the wave picture, in which energy should accumulate gradually and intensity should govern the kick.

Einstein's answer was one line: the maximum kinetic energy of an ejected electron is hν minus the work function of the metal. It carried no adjustable parameters — plot stopping voltage against frequency and you get a straight line of slope h/e, identical for every material.

Robert Millikan spent roughly a decade trying to break it. By 1916 he had instead confirmed the linear relation, the threshold and the universal slope, and had extracted a precision value of Planck's constant — 6.57 × 10⁻²⁷ erg·s, good to about half a per cent — from an experiment with nothing to do with thermodynamics. He continued to reject the light quantum anyway, writing that the reasoning behind the equation he had just verified "seems at present to be wholly untenable."

## Bohr's Atom and Its Four Failures

Rutherford's 1911 nuclear atom had a fatal defect: a circling electron is an accelerating charge, and classical electrodynamics says it must radiate and spiral into the nucleus almost immediately. In 1913 Niels Bohr's three-part paper in the *Philosophical Magazine* simply forbade this. Certain orbits were stationary and did not radiate; light was emitted only in transitions between them, with hν equal to the energy difference; angular momentum came in units of h/2π.

The reward was immediate. Balmer's empirical formula for the hydrogen lines fell straight out, and the Rydberg constant — until then a number read off a spectrograph — was now computable from the electron's charge and mass and Planck's constant.

The sharpest test came from a star. Peculiar lines in the spectrum of ζ Puppis were found at Harvard by Williamina Fleming and reported in 1896 by the observatory's director, Edward Pickering, who credited her with the discovery; the series carries his name regardless. Alfred Fowler later produced the same lines in the laboratory. Bohr identified them as singly ionized helium and predicted that its spectral constant should be exactly four times hydrogen's. Measurement said slightly more than four — about 4.0016. Bohr's reply was that the nucleus is not infinitely heavy: replace the electron mass with the reduced mass of the electron–nucleus pair, and the ratio comes out at 4.00163, matching the laboratory data. Fowler acknowledged it.

But the model could not handle neutral helium — two electrons and it yielded no usable ground-state energy. It said nothing about why some spectral lines are brighter than others. It failed on the anomalous Zeeman splitting. And it was philosophically incoherent, running classical mechanics for the orbits while banning classical electrodynamics by decree. It was, in the end, a scaffold.

## Standing Waves and an Island

Louis de Broglie's 1924 Paris thesis under Paul Langevin, *Recherches sur la théorie des quanta*, took the symmetry seriously: if waves carry momentum in packets, particles of momentum p should carry a wavelength λ = h/p. Bohr's angular-momentum postulate then stopped being a postulate. An orbit is permitted when its circumference contains a whole number of wavelengths — the integers arrive because standing waves must close on themselves. Einstein endorsed the thesis. In 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer scattered 54 eV electrons off a nickel crystal at Bell Labs and found a diffraction maximum at 50°; George Thomson, working in Aberdeen, diffracted electrons through thin metal foils. The 1937 Nobel Prize in Physics went jointly to Davisson and Thomson, "for their experimental discovery of the diffraction of electrons by crystals" — Germer, who had run the Bell Labs experiment at Davisson's side, was left out.

Werner Heisenberg spent about ten days on Helgoland in June 1925, and the legend of a single feverish night does not survive contact with the archive. Historians Anthony Duncan and Michel Janssen judge that he did much of the work before and after the trip; his June and July letters to Wolfgang Pauli show him unsure whether he had anything, and he sent the manuscript to Max Born to decide whether it was worth submitting. On 9 July 1925 he mailed Pauli the draft, with the target named plainly in the covering letter: "All of my pitiful efforts are directed at completely killing off the concept of orbits." *Zeitschrift für Physik* received the paper — the *Umdeutung*, or "reinterpretation" — on 29 July 1925.

Its move was to abandon the orbit entirely and keep only what a spectroscope registers: transition frequencies and intensities. Each dynamical quantity became an array indexed by pairs of states, and multiplying two of them meant summing over intermediate states — an operation whose answer depends on the order. Heisenberg found the non-commutativity disturbing. Born recognized it as matrix algebra, then almost unknown among physicists. Born and Pascual Jordan submitted the paper that made it rigorous on 27 September 1925, extracting the canonical relation pq − qp = (h/2πi)·I; the three-man paper with Heisenberg followed on 16 November.

## What Psi Means

Erwin Schrödinger disliked matrix mechanics and, in Zürich, built the alternative. *Annalen der Physik* received the first instalment of "Quantization as an Eigenvalue Problem" on 27 January 1926. He took de Broglie's waves literally and wrote a differential equation whose acceptable solutions in a Coulomb potential are those that stay finite and single-valued everywhere. The hydrogen energy levels emerged as eigenvalues — the integers appearing the way the overtones of a violin string do, from boundary conditions rather than decree. Planck called it "epoch-making work"; Einstein wrote that "the idea of your work springs from true genius."

Two theories now gave the same spectra with no shared vocabulary. In March 1926 Schrödinger published the bridge, showing that "to each function of the position- and momentum-coordinates in wave mechanics there may be related a matrix" obeying Born and Heisenberg's rules; Carl Eckart published an independent version in *Physical Review* that year. Later historians have questioned how airtight those arguments were — a fully general treatment waited for John von Neumann in 1932 — but physicists at the time took the point and stopped choosing sides.

What nobody had was a meaning for ψ. Schrödinger wanted a real matter wave, a smeared charge density. Born, working on collision processes, published the alternative in *Zeitschrift für Physik* in 1926: the squared modulus of the wave function is a probability density for the outcome of a measurement. The wave is real, but what it carries is odds. Born knew the cost and named it: "I myself am inclined to renounce determinism in the world of atoms. But that is a philosophical question for which physical arguments alone are not decisive."

## Brussels

Heisenberg's 1927 paper on the *anschaulich* — visualizable — content of quantum kinematics used a gamma-ray microscope to argue that pinning down an electron's position with short-wavelength light necessarily scrambles its momentum, giving δp·δq ~ h. Earle Kennard sharpened it the same year into the exact inequality on standard deviations, σ_p σ_q ≥ ℏ/2. Bohr objected to the derivation and made Heisenberg add a note in proof: the limit is not a photon knocking an electron about, but the impossibility of rigorously defining both quantities at once. On 16 September 1927, at the Volta centenary in Como, Bohr gave that idea its name — complementarity — in a lecture published in *Nature* the following April.

The fifth Solvay conference met in Brussels from 24 to 29 October 1927 under Lorentz, on the theme "Electrons and Photons"; seventeen of its twenty-nine participants were or became Nobel laureates. Einstein pressed thought experiments designed to beat the uncertainty relation, and Bohr's recorded reply is not the crisp riposte of legend: "I feel myself in a very difficult position because I don't understand what precisely is the point which Einstein wants to [make]. No doubt it is my fault."

Einstein's real objection was never squeamishness about chance. He had already written to Born on 4 December 1926 that "quantum mechanics is certainly imposing. But an inner voice tells me that it is not yet the real thing… I, at any rate, am convinced that He is not playing at dice." The load-bearing word is *yet*. His target was completeness, a thread that runs through the 1930 photon-box argument — which Bohr answered using Einstein's own gravitational time dilation — to the EPR paper of 1935.

And Solvay did not settle it. Guido Bacciagaluppi and Antony Valentini's reconstruction of the proceedings shows three live "lines of research" on the table — de Broglie's pilot-wave theory, Born and Heisenberg's quantum mechanics, Schrödinger's wave mechanics — sharply argued and unresolved. Twenty-seven years from Planck's Sunday visitors had produced a formalism nobody could fault and an interpretation nobody could agree on. A century later, only half of that has changed.
