## The Wreck Off Point Glyphadia

In the spring of 1900, sponge divers from Symi, under Captain Dimitrios Kontos, sheltered from a storm in the lee of Antikythera. They dived, and found a Roman-era cargo wreck 45 metres down off Point Glyphadia, strewn with bronze and marble statuary.

It became the first marine archaeological expedition in the discipline's history. The museum archaeologist Valerios Stais grasped what the divers had brought in and took them to his cousin Spyridon Stais, then Minister of Education; the state mounted an expedition for the winter of 1900–1901. Working that deep on surface-supplied air with no grasp of decompression, one diver died and two were paralysed.

An unremarkable corroded lump sat in a storeroom while the staff attended to the statues. On 20 May 1902 Spyridon Stais spotted a bronze fragment with gears in a box; the Athens papers of 22 May reported that he examined it with the museum's director, his cousin Valerios. Popular retellings compress this into one archaeologist making one discovery on 17 May.

Valerios Stais published the finds in 1905, dating the wreck to the middle of the first century BC. The familiar story has him recognising an astronomical clock; his own monograph more tentatively floats a maritime navigation instrument. The German philologist Albert Rehm was first to argue for an astronomical calculator. Most scholars refused it: a Hellenistic precision gear train was prochronistic, easier to set aside than to explain.

## The Differential Gear That Was Never There

Derek J. de Solla Price, a Cambridge-trained historian of science later at Yale, took an interest in 1951 and published "An Ancient Greek Computer" in *Scientific American* in June 1959. In 1971 he and the Greek nuclear physicist Charalampos Karakalos made X-ray and gamma-ray images of the fragments; the result was *Gears from the Greeks*, published in 1974 as volume 64, part 7 of the *Transactions of the American Philosophical Society*. Price described 27 gears in the main fragment, declined the attribution to Archimedes, and settled on an anonymous mechanician of Rhodes.

Then comes the part most accounts still get wrong. The centrepiece of Price's reconstruction was a differential gear, a train that adds or subtracts two rotations on a common axis: he took the large visible central wheel for a differential turntable producing 235 turns by subtracting 19 from 254. For thirty years encyclopaedias and histories of technology repeated the conclusion: the Greeks had invented the differential, fourteen centuries before Europe.

They had not. There is no differential in the Antikythera mechanism. Allan Bromley and Michael Wright refuted it from Karakalos's own radiographs, and found Price had apparently adjusted Karakalos's tooth counts to fit his model. Nor was it needed: ordinary fixed-axle gearing converts 254 to 235 without any wheel of extraordinary size. Price's boldest idea was a mistake — a productive one, since it made everyone else look harder.

Bromley, an Australian computer scientist, and Wright, a London museum curator, imaged the fragments with a linear-tomography rig in 1994. Bromley died in 2002; Wright had the plates digitised only in 2003. The pair, Tony Freeth later wrote, "took the first three-dimensional x-rays of the mechanism and showed that Price's model ... had to be wrong." Wright went on alone, built the first workable physical model, and in 2005 published the identification that mattered most: a pin-and-slotted follower in the lunar train.

## Eight Tonnes of X-Rays in Athens

In 2005 the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project — Cardiff, Athens and Thessaloniki, the National Archaeological Museum, Hewlett-Packard and X-Tek Systems — brought two non-destructive techniques to bear: Polynomial Texture Mapping, invented by Tom Malzbender at HP, which photographs a surface under many lighting angles and relights it in software to leave pure form; and microfocus X-ray tomography on an eight-tonne X-Tek machine nicknamed the Bladerunner, for which Athens police cleared the streets.

All 82 fragments went through both. The scans doubled the deciphered inscriptions, taking the total past 2,000 characters of Greek — much read for the first time from inside the corrosion — and resolved the gear trains well enough to publish: Freeth and sixteen co-authors, *Nature* 444, 587–591, on 30 November 2006, dating the device to around the end of the second century BC.

Thirty gears survive, 27 of them in Fragment A and one each in fragments B, C and D. The 2006 model of the Sun–Moon–eclipse machinery requires 37 meshing gears and accounts for 29 of the 30 that survive.

## What the Back Actually Says

Behind a removable back cover sit two large spiral dials, where the evidence is good: scale divisions and inscriptions survive. The upper dial is a Metonic calendar, 235 synodic months along a five-turn spiral: 235 lunar months run to within a whisker of 19 tropical years. Inside it are a four-year games dial and, more conjecturally, a 76-year Callippic dial.

The lower dial is an eclipse predictor on the Saros, the 223-synodic-month cycle inherited from Babylonian practice, laid out as a four-turn spiral of 223 cells. Fifty-one cells carry glyphs; on Freeth's 2014 reading these encode 38 lunar and 28 solar eclipse predictions, several cells carrying both. The glyphs record day and hour, plus direction of obscuration, magnitude, colour, the Moon's angular diameter and its relationship to the node.

The Saros runs 6,585 days and eight hours, so each repetition slips a third of a day. A subsidiary Exeligmos dial covers the 54-year triple Saros, its three sectors telling the user to add nothing, eight hours, or sixteen to the time the glyph gives. That is an error term, mechanised.

The 2008 follow-up in *Nature* identified the Metonic month names as Corinthian, prompting a suggestion of Syracuse, and so of Archimedes' heirs. Paul Iversen's 2017 study in *Hesperia* closed that door: the calendar is Corinthian in family but cannot be Syracusan, and is most likely Epirote, plausibly from Corinthian Ambrakia. Iversen also read the sixth set of games as the Halieia of Rhodes, pointing there for manufacture.

## The Pin and the Slot

The finest surviving component is the lunar anomaly device. Four gears — e5, e6, k1 and k2 — each carry 50 teeth. Gear e5 turns at the Moon's mean sidereal rate, from the relation that 254 sidereal months make 19 years: 254/19 rotations per year. It drives k1, mounted epicyclically on e3 rather than fixed to the frame. On the face of k1 is a pin; in the face of k2 is a slot; the pin rides in the slot. The axes are eccentric by just over a millimetre, so k2 turns unevenly, and that variation reaches the zodiac pointer through e6.

The elegance is in the carrier. Gear e3 rotates at the difference between the sidereal and anomalistic month rates — physically, the precession of the Moon's line of apsides — computed from the Metonic and Saros cycles together as (9 × 53)/(19 × 223) rotations per year. Because the assembly is carried around at that rate, the speed variation takes the period of the anomalistic month.

Popular accounts routinely say the pin and slot "corrects for the Moon's elliptical orbit". It does nothing of the kind: ellipses and Kepler's second law lie seventeen centuries in the future. What two 50-tooth wheels and a millimetre of eccentricity reproduce is the Greek deferent-and-epicycle theory of lunar motion, or the equivalent eccentre model — both compounded from uniform circular motions, both current in the mid-second century BC. Freeth and Alexander Jones wrote that it is "difficult to understand how this superbly economical mechanical design was conceived", and "by no means the obvious way" to model that theory.

## The Front, Which Is Mostly Argument

The front is a different evidential situation. A zodiac dial divided into 360 degrees survives, as does a calendar ring, with parapegma star-calendar plates above and below. Beyond that almost nothing remains: no planetary gearing survives at all, and every surviving gear is accounted for in the back trains except a 63-tooth wheel in Fragment D.

The case that planets were displayed rests on text, not metal. Improved readings of the back cover inscription, which described the machine's own displays, leave little doubt that revolving pointers carrying small spheres showed all five planets known in antiquity. The front cover inscription supplied two unexpected numbers, 462 for Venus and 442 for Saturn, implying period relations of (289, 462) and (427, 442), awkward figures any proposed gearing has to generate.

Reconstructions multiply. Wright built a physical planetarium with pointers for a true Sun and the five planets. In 2010 Evans, Carman and Thorndike read irregular spacing in the front-dial inscriptions as evidence for an off-centre solar indicator and simple per-planet dials rather than true pointers — far less gearing, and a job for the orphan 63-tooth wheel. Freeth and Jones proposed a scheme in 2012 modelled on the lunar pin-and-slot; Freeth's team published a full model in *Scientific Reports* in March 2021, with the caveat: "we cannot claim that our model is a replica of the original."

The calendar ring is unsettled too: it was long read as a 365-day Egyptian year. In 2024 Graham Woan and Joseph Bayley of Glasgow applied Bayesian inference, Markov Chain Monte Carlo and nested sampling — techniques from gravitational-wave astronomy — to the holes beneath the ring, which sit on a 77.1 mm radius with 0.028 mm mean radial scatter. Their 68 per cent credible estimate is 355.2 holes, or 354.1 excluding holes adjacent to fractures; 360 is strongly disfavoured and 365 not plausible. The likeliest reading is a lunar year of 354.

The dating is likewise live. The wreck sits around 70–60 BC; the 2006 team put the mechanism at the end of the second century BC. For the start-up epoch, Carman and Evans bounded the base Saros by the conjunctions of 28 April 205 BC and 9 May 187 BC; Iversen puts the calendar's start just after the new moon of 23 August 205 BC; Voulgaris and colleagues prefer the winter solstice of 23 December 178 BC. A machine that predicted eclipses to within a couple of hours has proved remarkably hard to date to within a century.
