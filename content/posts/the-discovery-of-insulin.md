On 11 January 1922 a boy in Toronto General Hospital was injected with a murky extract of ground-up ox pancreas. Leonard Thompson had been admitted on 2 December 1921 weighing sixty-five pounds and was held just short of death on 450 calories a day. He was thirteen, not the fourteen most accounts give; he was born on 17 July 1908.

The injection barely worked. His blood sugar dipped a little, sugar and ketones stayed in his urine, and a sterile abscess came up at an injection site.

Twelve days later he was injected again with a preparation made by a different man. His blood sugar fell to normal inside a day, the glycosuria and ketonuria disappeared, and he lived another thirteen years, dying of bronchopneumonia on 20 April 1935.

The gap between those two injections is where the discovery of insulin actually happened, and it is not the part usually told.

## What a diagnosis meant

For a child with what we now call type 1 diabetes, the years before 1922 offered one intervention: controlled starvation. Frederick Madison Allen, at the Rockefeller Institute, treated around a hundred patients between February 1914 and December 1917 and published seventy-six cases in *Total Dietary Regulation in the Treatment of Diabetes* (1919). The regimen opened with a fast of up to ten days, then 500 to 700 calories a day with a weekly fast day, raised toward 1,250 once the urine ran clear.

It bought time, and the price showed. Forty-three per cent of Allen's seventy-six cases were dead by late 1917; among patients admitted aged ten to nineteen the figure reached seventy-one per cent by February 1918, and among children under ten, seventy-five. Elliott Joslin, who used the same approach, put it plainly: "We literally starved the child and adult with the faint hope that something new in treatment would appear... It was no fun to starve a child to let him live."

## An idea built on a misreading

Frederick Banting, born 14 November 1891, was a twenty-eight-year-old surgeon in London, Ontario, with a thin practice and part-time work demonstrating surgery and anatomy at Western Ontario. Preparing a lecture on the pancreas, he read the Minnesota pathologist Moses Barron in the November 1920 *Surgery, Gynecology and Obstetrics*, on the islets of Langerhans in cases of pancreatic lithiasis. Barron had autopsied a case in which a stone had blocked the main pancreatic duct: the acinar tissue, which makes digestive juice, had wasted away; the islets had survived.

In the small hours of 31 October 1920, the date on the notebook page, Banting wrote: "Diabetus. Ligate pancreatic ducts of dog. Keep dogs alive till acini degenerate leaving Islets. Try to isolate the internal secretion of these to relieve glycosurea."

The reasoning was received wisdom. Researchers had been trying to extract the internal secretion since the 1890s and mostly failing; the standard explanation was that the digestive enzymes destroyed the hormone during extraction. Tie off the duct, let the acinar cells atrophy, and what remained would be islets you could grind up cleanly.

That premise was false — the part textbooks still get wrong when they present duct ligation as the insight that unlocked insulin. Trypsin leaves the acinar cell as inactive trypsinogen, switched on only in the intestine by enterokinase, so pancreatic tissue does not chew up its own hormone. Extraction in cold, slightly acidified alcohol — the method that worked — dissolves the hormone while leaving the enzymes inert. Duct ligation was never necessary. Banting had also read Barron against the grain: a pathological observation, not an extraction protocol.

He took the idea in early November 1920 to J. J. R. Macleod, Toronto's professor of physiology, author of *Diabetes: Its Pathological Physiology* (1913) and an authority on carbohydrate metabolism. Macleod warned it could end in the negative results that had defeated better men, then supplied laboratory space, dogs, two summer students, and instruction in the Hédon two-stage pancreatectomy.

## The summer of 1921

Charles Best and Edward Clark Noble were fresh from a Toronto degree in physiology and biochemistry. By Best's account they tossed a coin for the first stint; Best won and stayed the summer.

Work began on 17 May 1921 with a pancreatectomy. On 30 July they took the pancreas from dog 391 and injected the extract into the diabetic dog 410, whose blood sugar appeared to fall from 0.20 to 0.12 per cent in an hour. Dog 408, a yellow collie, got repeated doses of what they called "isletin" in early August.

Dog 33, Marjorie, had her pancreas out on 18 November 1921, received her first extract of fetal calf pancreas on 6 December, and lived roughly seventy days without a pancreas.

Buried in that summer is the experiment that should have ended the project. On 17 August 1921 an extract from fresh whole pancreas, with no ligation and no degeneration, worked about as well as extract from a degenerated one. The premise had been falsified by its own authors, who did not recognise it. By 8 December, when the whole pancreas of dog 35 gave a clean fall in blood glucose, the work could go forward on cheap whole glands from the abattoir. They still presented duct ligation as essential, and never acknowledged in print the experiments that contradicted it.

## The chemist

James Bertram Collip was an Alberta biochemist with a Toronto doctorate, in the city on a travelling fellowship. Around 12 December 1921 Macleod set him on purification, and everything that made insulin a drug happened in the six weeks that followed.

Collip worked the extract through graded concentrations of ethanol, kept cold and slightly acidic. At eighty per cent, unwanted protein and lipid precipitated out and were filtered off; at about ninety-five per cent the active material itself dropped out as a white solid. Late on 19 January 1922 he found the window — a way, as he wrote, "to get the active principle free from all the 'muck' with which it appeared to be inseparably bound."

He also rebuilt the measurement. Banting and Best assayed potency on depancreatised dogs: slow, expensive, surgical. Collip switched to ordinary rabbits and the Shaffer-Hartman micro-method, needing a few drops of blood from an ear. Potency testing became repeatable in an afternoon, which is what turns an extract into a dose. He was also first to describe hypoglycaemic overdose and its cure, and the conversion of blood glucose into liver glycogen.

The 11 January extract was Banting and Best's; the 23 January extract was Collip's. That is the whole difference between the failure and the recovery. Elizabeth Hughes, daughter of the American Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes, weighed forty-five pounds at her first injection on 17 August 1922, having lived on as few as 300 calories a day; five weeks later she had gained ten pounds.

## The method that went missing

In February or early March 1922 Collip could no longer make the stuff. He had never written the procedure down precisely, and patients already on insulin lost their supply while the group re-derived it. The likeliest culprit was mundane: variation in the laboratory's water pressure, which drove the vacuum pumps. They recovered the process by May, then handed scale-up to Connaught Laboratories and Eli Lilly. At Connaught, David Scott replaced much of the alcohol with cheaper acetone. At Lilly in November 1922 George B. Walden noticed insulin dropping out of solution at an unexpected pH and worked out isoelectric precipitation, yielding material ten to a hundred times purer.

The patent followed awkwardly. Banting thought it improper for a physician to profit from a drug and refused to sign, so Best and Collip, the non-physicians, filed. American law required the actual inventors be named; on 18 December 1922 Banting relented, and next day all three assigned their rights to the University of Toronto for one dollar.

## Credit

The quarrel started immediately. When Collip announced that Macleod had advised him to keep the purification method to himself until it was fully tested, Banting went for him; they came to blows and Best pulled them apart. On 25 January 1922 the university's J. G. FitzGerald imposed a written agreement against private patenting.

The first serious criticism came from outside. In the *British Medical Journal* of 16 December 1922 the Cambridge physiologist Ffrangcon Roberts dissected the Toronto papers and concluded that "the production of insulin originated in a wrongly conceived, wrongly conducted, and wrongly interpreted series of experiments." He was right, and Sir Henry Dale waved him away.

The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was announced on 25 October 1923 for Banting and Macleod. Banting, thirty-one, read it in a newspaper the next day, was furious Macleod was on it, and announced he would divide his half with Best; Macleod divided his with Collip. Neither Collip nor Best was among that year's nominees. Nicolae Paulescu, never nominated at all, protested on 6 November 1923: he had published on his extract, "pancréine", between April and August 1921, and Banting and Best's February 1922 paper reported his findings backwards, saying his injections into peripheral veins produced no effect when he had reported the opposite — a botched translation, Michael Bliss judged, rather than a theft. Even the name was second-hand: proposed by Jean de Meyer in 1909, popularised by Edward Sharpey-Schafer in 1916, and used in Toronto only once Macleod dropped "isletin".

Collip, who had the strongest grievance and made the least noise, said only that he was "quite happy to let the insulin story stand on the publications... by the group who collaborated in 1921-22." Bliss, whose 1982 archival history remains the standard account, settled on "glory enough for all."

That verdict is generous, and the wrong shape. The tidy version — young surgeon has an idea, tests it, saves millions — survives because it is the story we prefer. What the notebooks record is a hypothesis that was physiologically mistaken, pursued stubbornly enough to produce an active extract anyway; a disconfirming result its own authors read straight past; and a biochemist brought in at the end, who made the difference between an injection that raised an abscess and one that cleared a dying boy's blood in a day.
