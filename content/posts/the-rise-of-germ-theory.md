## The theory that was not stupid

Miasma theory held that epidemic disease rose as a poisonous vapour off rotting organic matter and that you caught it by breathing. Edwin Chadwick told a parliamentary committee in 1846 that "all smell is, if it be intense, immediate acute disease," and closed the thought with the four words that became the slogan of a movement: *all smell is disease.* The doctrine descends from Hippocrates, which is where a history like this usually starts sneering. Resist that. By the 1840s miasma had something better than antiquity behind it: a quantitative research programme that worked.

William Farr, who ran medical statistics for Britain's General Register Office, published a law of elevation in 1852: cholera mortality in London varied inversely with height above the Thames. He sorted the registration districts into terraces twenty feet apart, and the lowest ground carried around a hundred cholera deaths per 10,000 people against roughly a third of that on the highest. Low, wet, sewage-soaked ground bred the vapour; the hills were spared. The correlation was genuine and the predictions held. Meanwhile Chadwick's sanitarians drained cesspools and laid sewers, and over the long run mortality fell — because the filth that stank also carried pathogens. A theory that yields accurate forecasts and effective public works is not one a profession discards because an outsider produces an awkward table.

That is the intellectual weather in which the following five men worked. None of them converted the profession alone; between them it took thirty-five years.

## Vienna, 1847: correct answer, missing mechanism

The Vienna General Hospital ran two maternity clinics that admitted patients on alternating days — as close to a randomised trial as the nineteenth century managed by accident. The First Clinic was staffed by medical students, the Second by trainee midwives. Across 1840–46 the First Clinic lost 98.4 mothers per 1,000 births to puerperal fever; the Second lost 36.2. Women knew. They gave birth in the street to avoid admission on the wrong day, and Semmelweis described them begging on their knees not to be taken through the First Clinic's door.

Ignaz Semmelweis, an assistant in the First Clinic, got his answer from a death that was not a mother's. In 1847 his colleague Jakob Kolletschka was nicked by a student's scalpel during an autopsy and died; the post-mortem findings matched, organ for organ, what Semmelweis had been seeing in dead women. The students came to the delivery room from the dissecting room. The midwives did not. In mid-May 1847 he made handwashing in chlorinated lime compulsory before examinations. First Clinic mortality in April had been 18.3 per cent. June came in at 2.2, July at 1.2, August at 1.9. In 1848 the two clinics finished at 12.7 and 13.3 deaths per 1,000 — statistically indistinguishable.

So why was he not believed? Partly because he had no mechanism. Semmelweis blamed "decaying animal-organic matter," a poison, not a living thing that reproduced. That is not germ theory; it is miasma theory relocated from the air to the hands, and it made no prediction his opponents could not absorb into their own framework. His successor Carl Braun listed thirty causes of childbed fever and ranked cadaveric infection twenty-eighth — while continuing the chlorine washing, because it evidently helped, whatever the reason.

Partly it was that Semmelweis did not publish. His term under Johann Klein expired on 20 March 1849 and was not renewed, and the book — *Die Ätiologie, der Begriff und die Prophylaxis des Kindbettfiebers* — did not appear until 1861, fourteen years after the result, long and repetitive and furious. Rudolf Virchow rejected the doctrine at a congress of German physicians and natural scientists, and his authority did much of the damage. August Breisky pressed a real objection: Semmelweis had not shown that childbed fever and pyaemia were the same disease. James Young Simpson sent a stinging reply from Edinburgh to the effect that the British obstetric literature must be unknown in Vienna, since British obstetricians already treated the fever as contagious.

Then Semmelweis wrote open letters calling his critics murderers and telling the Würzburg obstetrician Friedrich Wilhelm Scanzoni that the history of childbed fever would not be unjust if it remembered him as a "medical Nero." He was committed to an asylum on 30 July 1865 and died there on 13 August, aged 47, of pyaemia from a gangrenous wound on his right hand — very likely the infection he had spent his life describing.

## Soho, 1854: the argument from the people who did not die

John Snow's cholera outbreak began on 31 August 1854 around a public pump on Broad Street; roughly 500 people died within ten days, 616 in all. The famous dot map is the least interesting part of his evidence. Snow examined the pump water on the evening of 3 September and found so little organic impurity in it that he hesitated over his own conclusion — the chemistry did not convict.

What convicted was the anomalies. The St James's Workhouse sat close to the pump with 530 inmates and had five cases, because it drew from its own well. The Lion Brewery's seventy men had none, because a daily beer allowance meant they drank no water at all. A widow who had moved to Hampstead still had Broad Street water carted to her; she died on 2 September, and a niece in Islington who drank from the same delivery died the following day. Snow put this to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish on the evening of 7 September, and the handle came off the following day.

Snow himself refused the tidy version. "The number of attacks of cholera had been diminished before this measure was adopted," he wrote, adding that he could not say whether they had fallen faster than the flight of the population would account for — the outbreak was already collapsing as Soho emptied. His stronger evidence was the south London comparison, where two companies supplied intermingled houses on the same streets: Southwark and Vauxhall drew Thames water below the sewage outfalls, Lambeth from further upriver at Thames Ditton. Over the first seven weeks of the epidemic, ending 26 August — before Soho went up at all — Southwark and Vauxhall houses recorded 1,263 cholera deaths among 40,046 houses, a rate of 315 per 10,000 houses, against Lambeth's 98 deaths among 26,107 houses, or 37 per 10,000. That was, in Snow's phrase, "between eight and nine times as great," in populations otherwise identical. The General Board of Health accepted the data and rejected the conclusion, attributing the epidemic to miasma. Farr held out until the East London outbreak of 1866, then produced the Old Ford Reservoir analysis that made him a convert.

## Paris, 1860: killing spontaneous generation, imperfectly

Snow's water needed something in it that multiplied. That required disposing of spontaneous generation, and here Louis Pasteur's opponent was Félix-Archimède Pouchet of Rouen, who had produced microbial growth in boiled hay infusions admitted to sterilised air under a blanket of mercury.

Pasteur's swan-necked flask is the elegant answer: boil the broth, draw the neck into an S-bend, leave the flask open. Air enters freely, dust settles in the bend, the broth stays clear indefinitely — and tip it into the neck and it clouds within days. He then went hunting the dust. In 1860 he opened flasks of sterile yeast water at sites of rising altitude: eleven in the Paris Observatory courtyard, all eleven spoiled; eight of twenty on the Jura plateau; and on the Mer de Glace, at some 2,000 metres, twenty flasks of which nineteen stayed sterile. He carried 73 of them to the Academy of Sciences that November, and at the Sorbonne on 7 April 1864 declared that the doctrine of spontaneous generation would never recover from the mortal blow of so simple an experiment.

The uncomfortable part: Pouchet's experiments were repeatable, and Pasteur's explanation of them — germ-laden dust on the surface of the mercury trough — was wrong in detail. Neither man knew that some bacteria form heat-resistant endospores. Hay infusions carry the hay bacillus, whose spores survive prolonged boiling; Pouchet was growing real survivors, not dust off the mercury. Pasteur's yeast infusions happened to carry none. The Academy's Alhumbert prize went to Pasteur in 1862; the commission appointed in January 1864 was stacked with his allies, and Pouchet withdrew in June rather than repeat his work before it. Only later, with John Tyndall and Ferdinand Cohn on spore thermotolerance, did the verdict rest on the reason it deserved.

## Glasgow and Wollstein: the method arrives

Joseph Lister read Pasteur and drew the surgical inference. He had also read that carbolic acid poured on Carlisle's sewage killed both the smell and the parasites that washed out into the pasture downstream. On 12 August 1865 an eleven-year-old named James Greenlees came into Glasgow Royal Infirmary with his tibia through the skin after a cart ran him down; Lister dressed the wound in lint soaked with carbolic, and when neat acid burned the boy's skin, diluted it in olive oil. Eleven cases later — nine recoveries, one amputation, and one death, and that death from haemorrhage rather than sepsis — he began publishing in *The Lancet* on 16 March 1867.

His statistics were thin and he said so: 16 deaths in 35 amputations for 1864–66, against 6 in 40 for 1867–70 — "no doubt, too small for a satisfactory statistical comparison." Critics went straight for the design. He supplied no ward figures at all for 1865, pleading that the hospital records were unfortunately imperfect for that year — and 1865 happened to be a year in which the Infirmary as a whole ran about 9 per cent mortality for upper-limb amputations, low by its own violently fluctuating standards. Hospital hygiene and operative technique had changed alongside the carbolic, so the historical control proved nothing about which change mattered; *The Lancet* urged repeatedly that one ward in a teaching hospital be set aside for antisepsis so that a concurrent comparison could be made, and none ever was. Lawson Tait published at least thirteen papers on the question between 1872 and 1890, and in 1882 reported a hundred ovariotomies with three deaths and none of the Listerian details at all. They were not being obtuse. Ordinary cleanliness worked too, and Lister's ritual was elaborate and expensive.

Robert Koch, a district physician in Wollstein with a home-made laboratory, supplied what everyone had been missing: a procedure. He grew *Bacillus anthracis* in hanging drops of ox aqueous humour on a warmed microscope stage and watched the rods throw "shiny egg-shaped bodies" — spores that survived, germinated, and explained why anthrax haunted particular fields for years. He passed the organism through some twenty successive generations of mice, each inoculation reproducing the disease and nothing else. In spring 1876 he demonstrated all of it over three days to Cohn and Julius Cohnheim at Breslau, and Cohn, having satisfied himself, ran Koch's paper — *Die Ätiologie der Milzbrand-Krankheit* — in the next issue of his own journal, *Beiträge zur Biologie der Pflanzen*.

Pure culture on solid media followed — agar reached the laboratory in 1881 by way of Fanny Hesse's suggestion to her husband Walther, Petri's dish in 1887 — and on the evening of 24 March 1882 Koch laid out the tubercle bacillus before the Berlin Physiological Society. The criteria he and Friedrich Loeffler set down in 1884, refined and published by Koch in 1890 and now called Koch's postulates, turned "germs cause disease" from a claim into a test anyone could run.

Conviction still lagged the evidence. Koch himself had to abandon the first postulate's universalism once he found cholera vibrios in healthy people, and typhoid carriers later made the same point. And on 7 October 1892, aged 73, Max von Pettenkofer — who conceded Koch's bacillus as a necessary ingredient but insisted the soil supplied the rest — neutralised his stomach acid with bicarbonate of soda and swallowed a broth culture of *Vibrio cholerae*, obtained from Koch, in front of witnesses. He got diarrhoea, shed vibrios for days, denied that it was cholera at all, and declared himself vindicated.
