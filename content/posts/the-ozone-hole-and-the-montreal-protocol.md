## Two chemists and a very stable molecule

In December 1973, Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland finished checking a calculation at the University of California, Irvine. His wife Joan asked each day how the work was going; once he trusted the numbers, his National Academy of Sciences memoir records the answer: "Good, but it might be the end of the world."

The calculation concerned chlorofluoromethanes: CFCl₃ and CF₂Cl₂, sold as Freon-11 and Freon-12 for refrigeration and aerosol propellant. Nothing much reacts with them, so where do they end up? Published in *Nature* on 28 June 1974, the answer was that they persist for 40 to 150 years, then drift into the stratosphere, where at 20 to 40 km they meet an ultraviolet window between 1,750 and 2,200 Å, a gap between the Schumann-Runge bands of oxygen and the Hartley bands of ozone, and photodissociate, releasing free chlorine.

What the chlorine does next is catalytic. Cl + O₃ gives ClO + O₂; ClO + O gives back Cl + O₂, regenerating the atom to run the loop again. At stratospheric temperatures ClO reacts with atomic oxygen about six times faster than NO₂ does, making chlorine far more efficient than the nitrogen oxides then under scrutiny.

Their conclusion was measured: the atmosphere, they wrote, "has only a finite capacity for absorbing Cl atoms produced in the stratosphere". A caveat in the same paper reads as prophetic: the calculations "have been based entirely on reactions in the gas phase, and essentially nothing is known of possible heterogeneous reactions of Cl atoms with particulate matter in the stratosphere."

## The industry's answer

DuPont, the largest producer, pressed Congress and the regulators to delay two to three years while the industry put three to five million dollars into research; a DuPont spokesman valued CFC-related industry at more than eight billion dollars a year and 200,000 American jobs.

The market moved faster. In June 1975 Johnson Wax took fluorocarbon propellants off its production lines; other brands followed, together placing as much as a quarter of DuPont's CFC business at risk. On testimony from Rowland, Molina, Cicerone and others, federal agencies banned CFCs as aerosol propellants in 1978. European governments rejected the American approach, and the unilateral ban cost US manufacturers an estimated 20 per cent of the global market: production shifted rather than stopped.

## A twenty-five-year-old instrument at Halley Bay

The British Antarctic Survey had measured total column ozone at Halley since the International Geophysical Year of 1957–58, with a Dobson spectrophotometer reading ozone from the ratio of two ultraviolet wavelengths.

In October 1982 Joe Farman, who ran the BAS geophysical unit, read values so far below anything in the record that his first hypothesis was the sensible one: the twenty-five-year-old instrument had drifted. The replacement he ordered agreed with it, and the readings kept falling.

Meanwhile Jonathan Shanklin, a Cambridge physicist who joined BAS in 1977 at 24 to digitise a backlog of hand-written Dobson sheets, was asked to help with an open day in 1983. With aerosol cans and Concorde in the news as ozone threats, he meant to reassure visitors by showing current data looked no different from twenty years earlier. It did, but springtime values looked lower year on year, and a graph of the minimum eleven-day mean showed the decline was systematic, running back to the late 1970s. By 1984 the October layer over Halley was about two-thirds as thick as in earlier decades.

Their *Nature* paper of 16 May 1985, "Large losses of total ozone in Antarctica reveal seasonal ClOₓ/NOₓ interaction", challenged the modelling consensus: assessments built on one-dimensional models for 30° N had suggested perturbations would stay small for another decade. Setting Halley against Faraday to the north-west, Farman, Brian Gardiner and Shanklin argued that temperatures holding low from midwinter until weeks past the spring equinox made the Antarctic stratosphere uniquely sensitive to inorganic chlorine, chiefly via the NO₂/NO ratio.

## What the satellites actually did

The standard telling, that NASA saw the hole for years and its software threw the readings away as instrument error, is wrong. The Ozone Processing Team flagged Total Ozone Mapping Spectrometer retrievals below 180 DU on reasonable grounds: nothing below 200 DU had been observed before 1983, and the sensor was not calibrated there. Flagged is not deleted; the values were kept. The team took up the anomalous October 1983 retrievals during 1984, and South Pole ground readings of a normal 300 DU made them ambiguous. By December 1984 they had submitted a conference abstract, five months before Farman's paper. The failure was siloed communication, plus a habit of zonal averaging that smears out a hole offset from the pole.

## Why Antarctica

Nothing in the 1974 chemistry predicted a hole: gas-phase models gave a few per cent of loss, spread thinly, decades out. Three explanations competed, and two failed. The dynamical one had late-winter sunlight heating the polar lower stratosphere and driving upward motion that lifted ozone-poor tropospheric air into the column; tracers showed the spring flow runs downward. The second invoked nitrogen oxides from the high solar activity of the early 1980s; measurements found a minimum of them over the south pole, not the maximum theory demanded. The third was chemical, and needed precisely what Molina and Rowland had flagged as unknown.

Susan Solomon and colleagues proposed in 1986 that reservoir species react on the surfaces of polar stratospheric clouds. Most stratospheric chlorine sits locked up as hydrogen chloride and chlorine nitrate, neither of which attacks ozone; on a cloud particle they react directly, HCl + ClONO₂ giving HNO₃ + Cl₂. The nitric acid stays behind; the chlorine leaves as Cl₂, which the first spring sunlight splits into two free atoms.

Antarctica supplies the clouds. Its winter vortex is colder and far more stable than the Arctic's, and satellites had shown clouds there from June to late September, between about 12 and 25 km, below roughly 200 K — Scott's diary for 1 August 1911 already records a "brilliant and most beautiful" iridescent cloud. Water ice needs the frost point near 188 K, but solid nitric acid trihydrate condenses well above it, widening the window. Larger particles sediment out, carrying the nitric acid away; that denitrification leaves too little NO₂ to recapture the chlorine in spring, so activation never shuts itself off.

One piece was missing — the piece textbooks most often get wrong. The classic cycle above needs a free oxygen atom, and there is very little of it at 15 to 20 km in polar spring, so it cannot be what empties the hole. Luisa and Mario Molina supplied the answer in 1987: two ClO radicals combine into the dimer Cl₂O₂, which photolyses and decomposes to release both chlorine atoms and an O₂. Two ozone molecules become three of oxygen, no O atom required. That cycle accounts for roughly 75 per cent of the removal inside the hole, a chlorine–bromine cycle for about 20 per cent more.

## The smoking gun

Solomon led the National Ozone Expedition to McMurdo in August 1986, cutting a hole in a hut roof so moonlight could reach a spectrograph. Visible spectroscopy of chlorine dioxide, OClO, revealed hundred-fold enhancements of active chlorine inside the vortex; a companion group there, using microwave emission, found ClO near 1 part per billion at 20 km, a hundred times what gas-phase theory allowed.

The decisive campaign was the Airborne Antarctic Ozone Experiment, flown from Punta Arenas, Chile, in August and September 1987: twelve ER-2 flights between 12.0 and 18.7 km, reaching 72° S along the Palmer Peninsula, plus thirteen of the DC-8. On 23 August James Anderson's in-situ ClO barely tracked ozone; by 16 September, as the aircraft crossed the vortex edge, the two were inversely correlated, ozone collapsing as ClO rose by about the right amount to explain the loss. The air inside was denitrified and dehydrated, as the cloud mechanism demanded; even so, the statement the team issued on 30 September called its findings preliminary.

## Montreal, and after

The diplomacy ran ahead of the smoking gun. The Vienna Convention, a framework with no binding limits, was adopted in 1985; the Montreal Protocol was signed on 16 September 1987, the same day as Anderson's decisive flight and before its data had been analysed. Its original terms were modest: developed countries would cut CFC consumption 20 per cent below 1986 levels by 1994 and 50 per cent by 1998, and freeze halon production at 1986 levels. What made it work was that it was built to be amended. London in 1990 required full phase-out of CFCs, halons and carbon tetrachloride by 2000 in developed countries and 2010 in developing ones; Copenhagen in 1992 pulled that forward to 1996; Montreal (1997) and Beijing (1999) tightened HCFC and methyl bromide controls; Kigali (2016) extended the machinery to hydrofluorocarbons, harmless to ozone but potent greenhouse gases.

## The honest arithmetic of recovery

Molina and Rowland warned in 1974 that "a lengthy period (of the order of calculated atmospheric lifetimes) may thus be required for natural moderation". Total tropospheric chlorine peaked at 3,660 parts per trillion in 1993 and had fallen only to 3,220 ppt by 2020, about 12 per cent below the peak: the molecules are exactly as durable as advertised.

The 2022 WMO/UNEP assessment projects a return to 1980 values around 2040 near-globally, around 2045 in the Arctic, and only around 2066 over Antarctica. The hole still opens every spring, and it is not an absence of ozone but a thinning: NASA marks it as the region below 220 DU, never crossed before 1979. After four deep, long-lasting holes from 2020 to 2023, 2024 broke the run with a peak ozone mass deficit of 46.1 million tonnes on 29 September, below the 1990–2020 average, its onset delayed by two rare Antarctic sudden stratospheric warmings in July and August. That later onset is among the more robust fingerprints of recovery, though the swings themselves reflect transport, not chlorine.

Because the controlled gases absorb infrared strongly, the Protocol is reckoned to avoid roughly 0.5 to 1 K of warming by mid-century against uncontrolled growth: a climate treaty by accident, signed by people arguing about sunburn.
