For fifteen hundred years, the accepted view was simple and unassailable: nature abhors a vacuum. Aristotle declared it, Galileo repeated it, and between them they accounted for every phenomenon where empty space seemed to appear — suction cups held by "horror vacui", wells drew water because nature refused to let the column gap open, pumps worked through the same refusal. Air had no weight. Nothing pulled or pushed. Empty space simply could not exist.

Evangelista Torricelli, who was 35 years old in 1643 and serving as chief mathematical assistant to the Grand Duke of Tuscany's court physicist, demonstrated that the whole edifice rested on a mistake so fundamental it would require a new word: atmosphere.

## Under Galileo's Shadow

Torricelli had replaced himself in an extraordinary appointment. In late 1642 Galileo asked his pupil to demonstrate how to cut a sphere into equal surface-area segments of specified proportions. The work impressed enough that by early 1643 Galileo brought Torricelli to Florence and placed him at the palace. Galileo died within weeks, on 8 January 1643. Less than two months later, on 2 April, the Grand Duke Ferdinando II de' Medici named Torricelli "Mathematician of the Grand Ducal Household" — a court position Galileo himself had held since 1639.

He was stepping into more than a title. Galileo's last investigations concerned the suction pump: what limited its lift, and whether the force that drew water upward could be overcome if the column were long enough. In his *Two New Sciences* of 1638, Galileo suggested that a vacuum might form above a raised water column, but stopped short of commitment — the "horror vacui" doctrine ran too deep to discard outright. "It is not impossible," he wrote with characteristic caution, "that nature might be exhausted before ten cubits."

Torricelli's task was experimental resolution. The court needed better pumps for draining flooded copper and gold mines at Ripa and Serravalle, 35 km southwest of Florence — water had to be drawn from up to 40 feet below grade. Water siphons topped out near thirty-three feet, a frustrating capstone that suggested either nature's refusal or an unknown mechanical limit.

## A Column That Falls Short

Instead of water, Torricelli switched to mercury. His reasoning was practical and elegant: mercury is roughly thirteen times as heavy as water. If the lifting force comes from whatever holds up a column of fluid, the same force should support only about one-twelfth the height in mercury compared with water — roughly 25 inches instead of 33 feet. A glass tube that long could sit on a workbench. The mine was too deep for convenient testing; the laboratory was not.

The experiment, carried out in Pisa, ran as follows. Take a glass tube about three feet long, sealed at one end. Fill it with clean mercury, removing every bubble by repeated inversion — trapped air would spoil the result. Invert it into an open dish also filled with mercury, keeping the closed end uppermost. Watch the column fall until equilibrium: the free surface of the mercury in the tube settles about 760 mm above the level in the dish. The sealed top holds empty space.

That was enough. The space at the top was vacuum — at least by modern standards a "Torricellian vacuum" containing only minute traces of mercury vapor. By Aristotle's physics, it should not exist. Galileo had stopped at "not impossible." Torricelli let it stand and explained what held the column up:

> We live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of the element air, which by unquestioned experiments is known to have weight.

The analogy was audacious. Just as seawater presses outward in a vessel sunk to depth, the "ocean" of air above presses downward on the mercury surface in the open dish, keeping the column suspended. The empty space at the top carries no counter-force. Day-to-day changes in the mercury level reflected weather — shifts in atmospheric pressure that could be predicted by monitoring the instrument.

This test was essentially the first documented barometer. Minor improvements followed: a bent-tube version with a scale, calibrated to millimeters, appeared within months. The unit named for him — one torr equals 1/760 of a standard atmosphere, or about 133 pascals — remains in use across vacuum engineering and meteorology.

## The Mountain Test

If air has weight and exerts pressure, then ascending should lower the mercury column: less atmosphere above means less push. Torricelli predicted this explicitly, but he never tested it himself. He died on 4 October 1647, only five days after publishing his *Opera Geometrica*, at age 39.

The experiment fell to Blaise Pascal — the mathematical prodigy who was then 35 himself and writing under the Latinized name "Pascalius." In March 1646, using a barometer of his own construction, Pascal performed what he called *Expériences nouvelles touchant le vide* — new experiments on vacuum. Indoor results convinced him, but only a mountain would settle it conclusively.

On 19 September 1648, Pascal arranged for his brother-in-law Florin Périer to carry two barometers to the summit of Puy de Dôme — a volcanic peak in the Auvergne region of France rising to 1,465 m above sea level. Périer took readings at the foothills (at Clermont), midway on the slope (at the Abbey of Orcet), and at the summit. The column was indeed shorter near the peak — by about 85 mm of mercury, roughly three inches lower than at normal ground level at Clermont below.

The result confirmed Torricelli's ocean-of-air model and sealed the argument against *horror vacui*. Air has weight, it presses with measurable force, and empty space is not impossible — it is precisely what forms wherever fluid retreats from a container because nothing inside pushes back. As Pascal summarized: "a child who knows addition and subtraction could do it."

## Why It Mattered

The barometer became instantly useful: weather prediction for the military, safe descent for mines, calibration of steam apparatus that would later power entire industries. Every reading of atmospheric pressure in a cyclone warning, altimeter setting, or vacuum gauge still traces its lineage to Torricelli's 1643 experiment in Pisa. But the philosophical impact was more immediate and more unsettling.

The experiment did two things simultaneously. First, it provided a concrete measurement of what had been invisible — air pressure as a force that could be captured in millimeters of mercury on a scale readable by anyone in the room. Second, it made vacuum not merely tolerable but routine and persistent. The space above the column was not transient; it did not collapse because nature objected. It lasted as long as the tube remained upright.

It took only one glass tube and some quicksilver — both inexpensive — to show that two thousand years of authoritative thought could be overturned by an experiment any serious workshop could repeat. Torricelli never received a formal academic appointment: he held only a court position, died young, and published little beyond *Opera Geometrica*. The barometer bore his ideas forward more effectively than any title could have done, and the torr remains his lasting inscription in quantitative form.

*The pressure unit "torr" is named after Torricelli: 1 torr = 1 mmHg exactly, so 760 torr defines one standard atmosphere. In SI units, standard atmospheric pressure equals approximately 1,013 hPa.*
