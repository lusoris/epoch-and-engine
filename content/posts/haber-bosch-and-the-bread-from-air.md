## The Arithmetic of Starvation

On 7 September 1898, William Crookes rose in Bristol to give the presidential address to the British Association for the Advancement of Science and told the wheat-eating nations they were going to run out of bread. His case was arithmetic. Bread-eaters were multiplying faster than new prairie could be broken, and land already under the plough was running down its stock of fixed nitrogen faster than manure could replace it. Within about thirty years — he settled on 1931 — demand would outrun supply.

The stopgap was Chilean caliche, the nitrate beds of the Atacama, worked at over a million tons a year and, by Crookes' reckoning, exhausted "in from twenty to thirty years." Then came the line that made the address famous: "It is the chemist who must come to the rescue of the threatened communities. It is through the laboratory that starvation may ultimately be turned into plenty." And the specific commission: "The fixation of atmospheric nitrogen therefore is one of the great discoveries awaiting the ingenuity of chemists."

The absurdity was that the raw material was everywhere. Air is more than three-quarters nitrogen — but held as two atoms joined by a triple bond of exceptional strength, inert and biologically useless until something breaks it: lightning, a root-nodule bacterium, or a machine nobody had built.

## Three Chemists Who Got It Wrong

Henry Le Chatelier saw that high pressure should favour ammonia, since the reaction packs four gas molecules into two, and in 1901 he tried it — until a severe laboratory explosion ended his interest in the problem.

Wilhelm Ostwald did worse. In 1900 he offered BASF a process passing nitrogen and hydrogen over heated iron wire at atmospheric pressure, claiming several per cent ammonia. BASF's chemists found the concentration was far beyond anything equilibrium allowed, traced the error, and Ostwald withdrew his patent application. As Max Appl notes in his history of the technology, this is one of the ironies of the field: iron was the right answer, reached for the wrong reasons and then abandoned.

Walther Nernst made the most respectable mistake. Haber's first systematic measurements in 1904–05, at atmospheric pressure and 1000 °C over iron, gave figures that were too high; the concentrations sat at the edge of what contemporary analysis could resolve. They did not fit Nernst's heat theorem, so Nernst ran his own experiments at 75 bar — the first ammonia work at elevated pressure — and got lower values. He was right about the thermodynamics and wrong about the engineering: a workable process, which he assumed must be once-through, was impossible, because the pressures needed lay beyond the means of the age.

## Karlsruhe, 1909

Haber's decisive idea was not pressure. It was the loop.

If you get only a few per cent conversion per pass, a once-through plant is hopeless. So Haber stopped optimising yield and started optimising rate — ammonia per cubic metre of catalyst per hour. Condense the product out under synthesis pressure, top the stream up with fresh gas, and recirculate the unconverted nitrogen and hydrogen with a compressor. He also anticipated preheating the feed against the hot reactor effluent. In 1909 this was a new way to think about a chemical plant, and it dissolved the objection that had stopped Nernst.

With Robert Le Rossignol, who built the high-pressure apparatus and valves, Haber filed his patent on the "synthesis of ammonia from its elements" on 13 October 1908. Early in 1909 he found that finely divided osmium gave 8 volume per cent ammonia at 175 bar and 600 °C. In July 1909 a bench-scale demonstration — a table-top machine dripping liquid ammonia at about 125 millilitres an hour — convinced BASF's representatives, and the board resolved to develop it with everything the company had. Carl Bosch, given extraordinary authority, took charge.

## Six Thousand Five Hundred Runs

Osmium worked and osmium was impossible: the world's entire stock ran to a few kilograms. Alwin Mittasch's job was to replace it, by brute force applied with unusual discipline. He built small test reactors with removable cartridges holding about two grams of catalyst and screened across nearly the whole periodic table. By 1910 he had tested more than 2,500 formulas in 6,500 runs.

The break came in November 1909, when magnetite from Gällivare in Sweden gave exceptional yields — striking because other magnetites were total failures. The active ingredient was therefore not the magnetite but whatever accompanied it. Mittasch went hunting those impurities deliberately, and by 1911 had the answer: iron with a few per cent alumina and a pinch of potassium, the substances now called promoters. The programme ran to 1922 to confirm the optimum; the only further gain was a little calcium.

Surface science later explained it. The rate-limiting step is the dissociative adsorption of nitrogen on iron. Alumina spaces the iron platelets apart so they cannot sinter into a low-area lump; potassium donates electronic charge that weakens the nitrogen-nitrogen bond and simultaneously eases ammonia off the surface so it stops blocking sites. None of that was known in 1911. Mittasch melted Swedish magnetite with additives, cooled it, crushed it and screened for particle size — and catalysts sold today are made much the same way, to much the same recipe.

## The Steel That Ate Itself

The catalyst was the chemistry problem. The steel was worse. Scaled up to reactors holding about a kilogram of catalyst, the vessels ruptured after only eighty hours.

Thin-section microscopy gave Bosch the explanation. The inner surface had entirely lost its tensile strength, and the damage had propagated outward from the bore until the sound metal remaining was too thin to hold pressure. The carbon steel had been decarburised — but what it left behind was not soft iron. It was hard and brittle. Hydrogen diffusing into the metal converted its carbon to methane; the methane, trapped at grain boundaries under enormous pressure, cracked the steel from inside. Systematic testing gave the general rule: all carbon steels are attacked under these conditions, and failure is only a matter of time.

Bosch's answer was to stop trying to resist it. He used a carbon steel pressure shell with a soft iron liner, soft iron having no carbide to lose. Hydrogen still permeated the liner, so he cut channels down its outer face and drilled small holes clean through the load-bearing shell to vent the accumulation to atmosphere. These became known as the Bosch holes. They did not measurably weaken the shell and the hydrogen lost was negligible. The high-pressure vessel at the heart of the process was deliberately perforated.

## Oppau, September 1913

The plant went up on a new site near the village of Oppau, now part of Ludwigshafen, rated at 30 tonnes a day. First production came in September 1913 and full capacity in 1914 — under five years from Haber's demonstration to a commercial process.

The flowsheet kept moving even then. Oppau commissioned on hydrogen from water gas separated cryogenically by the Linde–Frank–Caro process, which within months proved neither reliable nor economic at scale. It was displaced by the catalytic shift conversion W. Wild had found at BASF in 1912: pass the gas with surplus steam over iron/chromium oxide at 350–450 °C and the carbon monoxide reacts with water to yield more hydrogen. That step is still in every plant.

## The Saltpetre War

Germany had been the largest customer for Chilean nitrate, taking 37.9 per cent of the market in 1912. Admiral von Spee's squadron interrupted the shipping lanes until Sturdee destroyed it at the Falkland Islands on 8 December 1914; the trade then resumed for the Allies while the Royal Navy's blockade closed it to Germany. Nitrate is not merely fertiliser. It is the nitrogen in propellant and high explosive.

Oppau's ammonia, oxidised to nitric acid by Ostwald's own process, closed the gap — as ammonium nitrate, nitroglycerine and TNT. A second plant at Leuna came on stream in 1917. The 1914-1918-Online encyclopaedia records the conventional judgement: had it not been for the technical capacity to produce synthetic nitrogen, "the shortage of munitions would have cost Germany the war by 1915." Erisman and colleagues put it from the other side — Haber's discovery "fuelled the First World War, and, ironically, prevented what might have been a swift victory for the Allied Forces." A process invented against famine bought the Western Front three more years.

## Ypres

Haber did not merely supply the war. On 22 April 1915 the German army released 160 tons of chlorine from cylinders dug into the forward edge of its trenches near Ypres, into a light north-east wind. The French colonial troops facing it had nothing to breathe through; they died or fled, and the line opened over roughly six and a half kilometres. The 1st Canadian Division held the shoulder of the breach, and across the next forty-eight hours 6,035 Canadians — one man in three — became casualties, more than 2,000 of them killed. Haber was at the front in uniform, having spent weeks waiting on the wind.

His defence was that gas would shorten the war and was therefore the more humane weapon. There is little sign he interrogated the argument further.

Clara Immerwahr, his wife, had been the first German woman to take a doctorate in chemistry, in 1900; Haber proposed at a conference the following year. On the night of 1–2 May 1915, ten days after Ypres, with Haber home preparing to leave for the Eastern Front to organise the next attack, she wrote farewell letters, took his army revolver into the garden, fired a test shot and shot herself. He left for the front the next morning. Her reasons are genuinely contested — the documentation is thin and what she left behind was destroyed — but the sequence of dates has never come apart.

Haber took the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the synthesis of ammonia from its elements, over loud objection; Bosch shared the 1931 prize for high-pressure methods. In 1933 the Nazi racial decrees made Haber's position untenable at the institute he had built. He left Germany and died in 1934, looking for a post in Palestine.

## The Ledger

The reaction has not fundamentally changed since Oppau. Almost every ammonia plant still runs hydrogen and nitrogen over promoted iron at 400–500 °C above 100 bar and recycles what does not convert; the ruthenium catalysts commercialised from 1990 are the only real departure.

What it bought is measurable. Erisman and colleagues calculate that humans supported per hectare of arable land rose from 1.9 to 4.3 between 1908 and 2008; nitrogen fertiliser fed 44 per cent of the world in 2000 and 48 per cent in 2008, so that "the lives of around half of humanity are made possible by Haber-Bosch nitrogen." On a world population now past eight billion, that is roughly four billion people alive who otherwise would not be. It is also the origin of the sharper version of the claim: if half the world's food grows on synthetic nitrogen, then for someone on an average modern diet something close to half the nitrogen atoms in their muscle, enzymes and DNA entered the living world through a steel converter rather than a root nodule.

The bill is measurable too. About 100 teragrammes of Haber-Bosch nitrogen went into global agriculture in 2005, of which only 17 reached people as crop, dairy and meat. Of the nitrogen lost to the environment, roughly two-fifths is denitrified harmlessly back to inert atmospheric nitrogen; the rest leaks into groundwater, rivers, coastal algal blooms and the air as nitrous oxide and ammonia. Fixing it costs at least 32 megajoules per kilogram of nitrogen, on the order of one per cent of global primary energy. And the same authors draw the other line explicitly, tying the ammunition that reactive nitrogen made possible to 100–150 million deaths in twentieth-century armed conflict.

Crookes asked chemistry to turn starvation into plenty. It did exactly that, on schedule, in a converter with holes drilled through its pressure shell — and delivered the second product in the same package, because nitric acid does not care what it is destined for.
