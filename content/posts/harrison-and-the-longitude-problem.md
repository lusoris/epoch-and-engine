## A fleet on the rocks

On the evening of 22 October 1707, five ships of Sir Cloudesley Shovell's homeward-bound Mediterranean squadron struck the rocks off the Isles of Scilly. Within hours four had gone down and more than 1,400 men were dead, the Admiral of the Fleet among them.

The wreck is usually told as the shock that forced Parliament's hand. Royal Museums Greenwich is blunt: navigational error was the cause, but "there is no evidence of a public outcry urging government action. It seems instead that the Longitude Act was the result of lobbying by mathematicians like William Whiston." Whiston and Humphry Ditton, pushing a scheme of moored signal ships firing shells at known times, petitioned Parliament; that, not the drowned sailors, produced the committee.

## What the Act actually asked for

In July 1714 Queen Anne assented to "An Act for Providing a Publick Reward for such Person or Persons as Shall Discover the Longitude at Sea". On the advice of Newton and Halley the reward came in three tiers: £10,000 for a method good to one degree, £15,000 for forty minutes of arc, £20,000 for half a degree. Candidates had to prove it on a six-week voyage to a West Indies port.

Longitude is time in disguise. The Earth turns 360 degrees in twenty-four hours, so each degree is four minutes of clock time and half a degree is two minutes: over six weeks, about 120 seconds of error in all. This is where the story is usually told wrong. That figure demands not a clock that keeps perfect time but one whose *rate* is predictable. A machine gaining twenty seconds a day is usable: the gain is measured before sailing and subtracted afterwards. What ruins a navigator is a rate that wanders with heat, damp, salt and the motion of a ship.

Nor was the obstacle raw precision: good land regulators already beat three seconds a day, Harrison's own wooden clocks among them. The obstacle was the sea. A pendulum needs a stable platform and a constant gravity; a rolling ship at a changing latitude offers neither. The Board of Longitude saw nothing worth its attention for twenty-three years.

## The astronomers' answer

What finally roused it was a joiner from Barrow upon Humber. But the clock was never the favourite: the rival was lunar distance.

The Moon moves about thirteen degrees a day against the fixed stars, a slow hand on a celestial clock. Measure its angle from the Sun or a catalogued star, correct for refraction and parallax, compare against tables predicting that angle at Greenwich, and the difference gives Greenwich time. The theory was old; the tables were the problem. Tobias Mayer of Göttingen supplied them, improved by Leonhard Euler, and the Board eventually paid Mayer's widow £3,000 and Euler £300.

Maskelyne tested the method at sea. Sent to St Helena in 1761 for the transit of Venus, he reported his longitude by lunars "only 1½° in error compared to errors of up to 10° from dead reckoning". The catch was arithmetic: each sight demanded a long chain of corrections. His answer, an idea from Lacaille he met in the French *Connaissance des temps*, was to precompute everything at three-hourly intervals. Appointed Astronomer Royal in February 1765, he had the first *Nautical Almanac*, for 1767, ready by the end of 1766. A lunar now cost perhaps half an hour with a sextant and a book.

A sextant and an almanac cost a few pounds against £450 for Kendall's later copy of Harrison's watch, and only astronomical observation could tell a captain whether his timekeeper still kept time. The two were complements.

## Four machines

H1 was built at Barrow upon Humber between 1730 and 1735: brass, bronze, oak and lignum vitae, 620 millimetres tall. Its ancestry lay in Harrison's wooden longcase clocks, which had already yielded the gridiron pendulum — alternating brass and iron rods whose unequal expansions cancel — and the grasshopper escapement, which released the driving power almost frictionlessly and needed no oil.

At sea a pendulum is useless, so H1 used two dumbbell balances linked so the ship's motion affected them equally and oppositely. The 1736 trial was not the transatlantic run the Act required: Harrison sailed to Lisbon in HMS *Centurion* and returned in HMS *Orford*. On the return leg the timekeeper placed the landfall at the Lizard, sixty miles from the ship's master's reckoning. The Board met in June 1737 and voted Harrison £500, £250 of it at once.

H2 (1737–39) added a remontoire — a secondary spring rewound at intervals so the balances felt a uniform push, not the mainspring's fluctuating pull. It never went to sea: Harrison realised a bar balance changes *period* when a ship yaws.

H3 took nearly nineteen years and never worked well enough: its circular balances were immune to yaw, but Harrison could not make them isochronous, because nobody yet understood the springs controlling them. It was still horology's most fertile failure. To compensate for temperature he laminated two metals of different expansion into a strip that bends when warmed — the bimetallic strip, "still in use worldwide in thermostats of all kinds". To kill friction he caged his rollers apart, inventing "the caged roller bearing, a device found in nearly all modern mechanical engineering".

Then came the machine that changed his mind. Around 1752–53 the London watchmaker John Jefferys built a pocket watch to Harrison's design, with temperature compensation and maintaining power. It kept time far better than a watch had any business doing: a small balance beating fast is dominated by its own momentum, not by the ship. H4, finished in 1759, is a silver-cased watch 13 centimetres across, ticking five times a second, with diamond pallets two millimetres long, a remontoire rewound every seven and a half seconds, and a bimetallic "Thermometer Kirb" bending the curb pins as temperature changed.

## Two voyages and a chair

HMS *Deptford* sailed from Portsmouth on 18 November 1761 with Harrison's son William and the watch. Its rate was fixed beforehand at 24⁄9 seconds lost per day. At Jamaica, after subtracting the 3 minutes 36.5 seconds that rate predicted over eighty-one days, H4 was five seconds slow — 1.25 minutes of longitude, about one nautical mile. That subtraction *is* the method: the watch was never asked to run true, only predictably. The Board suspected luck, and doubted the practicality of a device that had taken six years to build.

The 1764 trial was three-cornered. Maskelyne had left Portsmouth on 9 September 1763 with his assistant Charles Green to test lunars and fix Bridgetown's longitude from Jupiter's satellites; William Harrison followed in HMS *Tartar* in March. The third candidate, Christopher Irwin's marine chair — a gimballed seat for observing Jupiter's moons from a rolling deck — Maskelyne found useless. The other two passed: H4 kept time to within 39 seconds, under ten miles of error, and lunars came within about thirty.

## Judge and witness

The 1765 Act split the money: £10,000 for disclosing his principles, £10,000 more once the watch was shown to be replicable. Harrison disclosed on oath to six gentlemen appointed by the Board, finishing on 22 August 1765 — with Maskelyne attending because he "insisted on having a Right to attend, as being a Commissioner". H4 went to Greenwich that October for a ten-month trial under the Astronomer Royal.

Maskelyne reported that "the Watch was getting from the very first near 20 seconds per day", and inferred that H4 "cannot be taken to pieces and put together again without altering its Rate of going considerably". His verdict: the watch "cannot be depended upon to keep the Longitude within a Degree, in a *West-India* Voyage of six Weeks".

Harrison's reply accused him of "the Suppression of some Facts and the Misrepresentation of others", called him "deeply interested in the Lunar Tables, a Scheme set up some Years ago for the Reward in Competition with my Invention", and restated the principle Maskelyne had missed: a watch need not run perfectly, only "invariably perform it in *some known Time*". The same pamphlet supplies the explanation. Believing the Board wanted only formal delivery, Harrison had kept the dismantled watch, "went on making some experiments, and alter'd the Rate of its going", then surrendered it that October without anyone asking the new rate. Each was right about something: the gain was Harrison's own doing, not a fragile mechanism — but he had handed over an instrument he had quietly re-rated.

## Was he cheated?

Less than the legend says. The structural fault was real — the Board's chief expert witness was also one of its judges — but the picture of a lone genius robbed by a jealous villain is a modern invention. Maskelyne never entered a method of his own for reward, treating the *Almanac* as the duty of his office, and the very pamphlet Harrison tore apart called H4 "a useful and valuable Invention" that, with lunar observations, "may be of considerable Advantage to Navigation". Harrison put it best himself: "Here are now two Methods before the Public; Wou'd to God there were two Hundred!"

Replication came from Larcum Kendall, who had trained under the same John Jefferys. His copy K1 cost £450, was finished in 1769, and went with Cook on his second and third voyages; Cook called it his "never failing guide". George III tested H5 himself over ten weeks in 1772 at Kew and found it good to a third of a second a day. In 1773 Parliament voted the eighty-year-old Harrison £8,750.

He never received the prize as written; nobody ever did. He received £23,065 across thirty-six years — £4,315 in instalments from the Board, £10,000 in 1765, £8,750 in 1773, the last two alone worth over £3 million by the Office for National Statistics' reckoning — more than any other claimant, from a Board that had funded him since 1737. The grievance was real; so was the money. What lasted is neither: it is the bimetallic strip, the caged roller bearing, and the discovery that a small fast balance beats a big slow one at sea.
