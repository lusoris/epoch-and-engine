At 11:02 on the morning of 7 November 1940, a six-hundred-foot section of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge tore free of its suspender cables and fell a hundred and ninety-five feet into Puget Sound. The bridge had been open four months and six days. The wind that destroyed it blew at about 42 miles per hour — unremarkable for the Narrows, roughly a third of the 120 mph it was designed to survive. The footage is the most-watched in engineering, and the caption attached to it is usually wrong.

## The Theory That Made It Thin

Leon Solomon Moisseiff (1872–1943) was the most influential suspension-bridge engineer in America. He had brought the Austrian Josef Melan's deflection theory into American practice with the Manhattan Bridge in 1909. Its insight was that in a long span the deck's own dead weight supplies most of the stiffness, so the stiffening trusses standard since Roebling did far less work than anyone assumed. In 1933 Moisseiff and Frederick Lienhard extended it to wind, reckoning the main cables would absorb up to half the static wind pressure on a deck. Lighter, shallower decks were now justified.

Washington's highway department had a conventional crossing by its bridge engineer Clark Eldridge, with trusses 25 feet deep, estimated at $11 million. The Public Works Administration judged that excessive; Moisseiff said he could cut it to $7 million with solid plate girders 8 feet deep. It approved nearly $6 million on 23 June 1938; the final cost was $6.4 million.

What opened on 1 July 1940 ran 2,800 feet in the main span — third-longest in the world — with 1,100-foot side spans and two lanes on a deck just 39 feet between cables. Depth-to-span was 1:350 and width-to-span 1:72: against the Golden Gate, half again as slender in plan, more than twice as shallow in section, and a centre span some three and a half times lighter. Moisseiff's girders were not lattice trusses full of holes but a continuous eight-foot blade of steel.

## Galloping Gertie

It moved before it was finished. Ammann's board recorded four-foot vertical oscillations at sixteen cycles a minute in winds of three or four miles an hour — and days of stillness in 35 mph. Crews gave it the name that stuck; drivers paid the toll for the sensation. Nobody read it as a warning: the profession assumed wind loaded a bridge statically.

Three fixes were tried. Tie-down cables anchored to fifty-ton concrete blocks snapped within days. Inclined stays from the main cables to the deck at mid-span were still in place when the bridge fell, and had made no difference. Hydraulic buffers lost their seals when the bridge was sandblasted before painting.

The state hired Frederick Burt Farquharson of the University of Washington to find out why. He and his students built a 1:200 model of the bridge and a 1:20 deck section, concluding their first studies on 2 November 1940. He offered two remedies: drill holes through the girders so air could pass, or bolt on fairings to streamline the cross-section. The first was rejected as irreversible; the second was approved days before the collapse and never installed.

## The Morning of 7 November

Wind measured 38 mph at 7:30 a.m. and 42 mph by 9:30. Eldridge crossed at 8:30; Farquharson drove down from Seattle at 9:30 with a camera. The centre span was running eight or nine nodes at 36 to 38 cycles per minute — vigorous, but the motion everyone knew.

Just after ten it changed. A cable band at mid-span on the north cable slipped, splitting it into two unequal segments; the federal report's preface calls this the first failure, one that "probably initiated the torsional oscillations." The deck stopped heaving and began to twist, the many nodes collapsing into one at mid-span while the roadway's two halves rotated in opposite directions about a motionless centreline.

The frequency dropped to 0.2 hertz — Farquharson timed twelve cycles a minute on a stopwatch — and the amplitude did not stop climbing. By 10:07 the deck was tilting through angles approaching 45 degrees at the quarter points, with cable amplitudes near 28 feet. Leonard Coatsworth, a Tacoma News Tribune editor, abandoned his car and his daughter's black cocker spaniel Tubby and crawled off on hands and knees. Farquharson reached the car at 10:55, opened the door to coax the dog out, and was bitten. Tubby was the collapse's only casualty.

Barney Elliott and Harbine Monroe filmed it too — Elliott at 16 frames per second, Monroe at 24. Because copies were printed as though all of it were shot at 24, the footage most people know runs half again too fast: eighteen twists a minute against the stopwatch's twelve.

## Why It Was Not Resonance

The classroom account says vortices peeling off the deck matched a natural frequency of the bridge, like a child timing pushes on a swing, until the amplitude ran away. The numbers refuse it. A bluff body sheds a Kármán vortex street at a frequency set by the Strouhal relation, f = S·U/D. Billah and Scanlan put this deck's depth D at 8 feet and its Strouhal number S at about 0.11: in a 42 mph wind, shedding close to 1 hertz. The destructive mode ran at 0.2 hertz, five times slower — and that figure was no mystery either: it was the bridge's own fundamental antisymmetric torsional mode, which Farquharson had found on a 1:50 model. Nothing in the wind was pushing at that rate, and the bridge needed nothing to.

The real mechanism needs the aerodynamic force to depend on the bridge's own motion. Forced resonance has an external, fixed driving term — mẍ + cẋ + kx = F sin(ωt) — and needs ω near the natural frequency. Scanlan's torsional equation has none: Iθ̈ + [c − c_a(U)]θ̇ + kθ = 0 is homogeneous, with no independent function of time in it. The aerodynamic moment splits into a part proportional to the twist angle, which shifts the stiffness, and a part proportional to the *rate* of twist, which acts as damping and grows with U. Below a critical speed net damping is positive and disturbances die; above it, damping goes negative and perturbations grow exponentially. The wind supplies the power; the motion works the tap.

Nor was this aircraft flutter, which couples bending and torsion and arrives violently. Over a bluff deck the flow separates instead of leaving cleanly at a trailing edge, giving separated-flow flutter in one degree of freedom that builds gradually: the old bridge took forty-five minutes to die. Resonance would have been a peak, detuning either side of one wind speed; flutter is a threshold, and section-model tests later put this deck's near 18 to 27 mph — far below the 42 mph blowing that morning.

## What the Carmody Board Actually Said

The Federal Works Agency appointed Othmar Ammann, designer of the George Washington Bridge; Theodore von Kármán, whose name the vortex street carries; and Glenn B. Woodruff, a Golden Gate consultant. Their report to administrator John M. Carmody is dated 28 March 1941. It blamed excessive flexibility, noted that the solid plate girder and floor had behaved like an aerofoil, and held that no suspension design should proceed without wind-tunnel tests.

On vortex resonance it was blunt and correct: "It is very improbable that resonance with alternating vortices plays an important role in the oscillations of suspension bridges," because "there is no sharp correlation between wind velocity and oscillation frequency such as is required in case of resonance with vortices whose frequency depends on the wind velocity."

But the right answer was not in the report. The same document found "no convincing evidence that the vertical oscillations were caused by so-called aerodynamic instability," offering only that "at the higher wind velocities, torsional oscillations, when once induced, had the tendency to increase their amplitudes" — the symptom, not a name for the disease. Von Kármán himself, in a biography written with Lee Edson, is quoted blaming "the Karman Vortex Street." The board had eliminated the wrong answer without stating the right one. Robert Scanlan and John Tomko named it in 1971: single-degree-of-freedom torsional flutter. Billah and Scanlan carried the case to physicists in the *American Journal of Physics* in February 1991, naming Resnick and Tipler among the texts at fault and attributing the collapse to "an aerodynamically induced condition of self-excitation or 'negative damping' in a torsional degree of freedom."

The board refused to blame Moisseiff, and David Steinman and Sara Watson put it plainly: the failure "is not to be blamed on him; the entire profession shares in the responsibility," which had "neglected to combine, and apply in time, the knowledge of aerodynamics and of dynamic vibrations with its rapidly advancing knowledge of structural design." Steinman's own Thousand Islands (1938) and Deer Isle (1939) bridges were narrow, flexible spans that oscillated badly and needed retrofitted cable ties. Moisseiff died in 1943 with the collapse attached to his name.

## Afterwards

Farquharson spent 1941 to 1947 in the wind tunnel; Charles E. Andrew consulted on the replacement, Dexter R. Smith was its chief design engineer, and the new span inverted every choice that had killed the first: Warren stiffening trusses 33 feet deep instead of 8-foot plate girders, a deck 60 feet wide instead of 39, depth-to-span of 1:85 instead of 1:350, and open steel grating between the lanes. Testing gave confidence it would stand to 127 mph; it opened on 14 October 1950 and still stands. Section-model wind-tunnel testing became a condition of federal funding, and long spans reverted to deep, open trusses until the Severn Bridge's aerofoil-section steel box girder in 1966.

One question stayed open. Allan Larsen argued in 2000 that Billah and Scanlan "fail to connect the vortex pattern to the shift of apparent section damping from positive to negative, which signifies the onset of torsional instability" — they had described the runaway without explaining what began it. His own answer was the formation and drift of large vortices across the deck. Self-excitation amplifies a rotation that already exists; what tipped a bridge that had merely galloped for four months into rotating at all remains contested, the slipped cable band the leading candidate. Tacoma Narrows is a settled lesson in what destroyed it and an unsettled one in what began it.
