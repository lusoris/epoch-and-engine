At the end of the 1850s a ton of Sheffield cast steel sold for £50 to £60; Bessemer's own firm, tendering to Woolwich in June 1859, quoted exactly that as the trade price. English pig iron cost about a tenth of it. The gap was labour, fuel and waiting, and it decided what the century built. Rails were wrought iron because steel rails were an extravagance.

## Ten Days in a Charcoal Chest

Bessemer described the trade he was about to wreck exactly. Converting Swedish bar iron into crude steel, he wrote, "occupied about ten days": two days and nights heating the furnace, the bars "carefully packed in large stone boxes with a layer of charcoal powder between each bar"; six days at a white heat; two more to cool.

That is cementation: carbon soaking slowly and unevenly into solid iron, raising the surface blisters that named blister steel. Thick bars could sit a week at heat and need a fortnight to cool, and the biggest works treated up to sixteen tons a charge. Melting came second. From about 1740 Benjamin Huntsman remelted blister bars near Sheffield in clay pots of roughly 15 kg at some 1,600 °C, which averaged the carbon out.

Sheffield saw no threat. It "wrapped itself in absolute security", Bessemer recalled, and laughed at "the absurd notion of making five tons of cast steel from pig-iron in twenty or thirty minutes, when by its own system fourteen or fifteen days and nights were required to obtain a 40-lb. or 50-lb. crucible".

## A Fire With No Fuel

The idea was not a better furnace but the abolition of the fuel bill. Pig iron carries four to five per cent carbon along with silicon and manganese, and those impurities burn: blow air through the melt and it heats itself on its own contamination.

Bessemer's premise was "that carbon cannot exist at a white heat in the presence of oxygen without uniting therewith and producing combustion", and that a "vast surface" of it exposed to oxygen would produce heat "hitherto unattainable". Cold air does make the iron hotter — but that reason is largely wrong, and the error is still repeated. Silicon oxidises first and yields far more heat per kilogram than carbon; with manganese it supplies most of the energy that carries the bath past the melting point of steel. Carbon goes afterwards, burning as carbon monoxide in the flame at the mouth.

## Cheltenham, and £27,000 in a Month

Bessemer left London on 12 August 1856 and read his paper to the British Association next morning, under its actual title: "The Manufacture of Iron Without Fuel." *The Times* printed it in full.

On 27 August the Dowlais trustees H. A. Bruce and George Clark arrived in London. They declined a demonstration: their chemist E. Riley had already extemporised a converter in a shed and tried it. They agreed to pay £10,000 for the right to make 20,000 tons of malleable iron a year at a farthing per ton, against the ordinary royalty of ten shillings.

Three more followed: "Mr. Smith Dixon, of the Govan Iron Works, Glasgow", on identical terms; Butterley for 10,000 tons; a Welsh tin-plate maker for 4,000 tons and £2,000. That made "sales of royalties to the amount of £27,000 in less than one month" — every licence for malleable iron, not steel.

## The Bolt from the Blue

Then the licensees fired their converters. Small upright fixed vessels went up at Galloway's in Manchester, at Dowlais, at Butterley and at Govan, "and in each case the results of the trials were most disastrous". Ordinary bar-iron pig "was found to contain so much phosphorus as to render it wholly unfit... This startling fact came on me suddenly, like a bolt from the blue."

Phosphorus makes iron cold-short — brittle worked cold — and the blow did not remove it. Bessemer and his partner Robert Longsdon repurchased the licences for £32,500, "or £5500 more than they were sold for".

Two accidents explain why his own trials had worked. "By the mere accident of living in London," he wrote, a jobbing founder had sent him "the grey Blaenavon iron" — Welsh metal low enough in phosphorus to behave. The second was the lining: he used "clay or firebrick, and not with a silicious material such as ganister or sand", and later blamed his licensees' silica for having "prevented the elimination of any phosphorus". That flatters him. Firebrick is an aluminosilicate, acid to neutral — not a basic lining, and unable to lock phosphorus into a stable slag. He had been handed clean iron.

## Mushet's Triple Compound

A second defect remained. Twenty minutes of air leaves the metal not merely decarburised but burnt: saturated with dissolved oxygen, and unforgeable. Robert Forester Mushet, at his Forest Steel Works in the Forest of Dean, supplied the fix. Blow everything out, then add back a weighed dose of spiegeleisen — a manganese-rich cast iron, about 8.5 per cent manganese and 5.25 per cent carbon in Mushet's own analysis. The manganese scavenges the oxygen into the slag; the carbon returns to specification. He filed his provisional specification on 22 September 1856.

He was not rewarded. His patent lapsed, he was destitute by 1866, and his sixteen-year-old daughter Mary went to London alone to confront Bessemer. Told that he used "what your father had no right to claim", she left with a cheque for £377 14s 10d. Bessemer later settled £300 a year on Mushet, "aiming at abating an intolerable nuisance which I had no other means of preventing", paying until Mushet died in 1891, "having thus paid him over £7000". The Iron and Steel Institute disagreed, and gave him its Bessemer Gold Medal in 1876.

## What the Steel Then Cost

Bessemer's Sheffield works opened with "the first converter mounted on axes" in 1858, quoting tool steel at "£42 per ton, as against £50 or £60 by other makers". Woolwich refused four tenders in 1859, from £42 down to £32; when the firm quoted £20 on 7 December and won the order, the department condemned the whole delivery on the evidence of broken chisels made from a bar size absent from the contract.

Bessemer steel rails cost £22 a ton in 1861. Three decades on, Bessemer put the average price of rails across 1891 and 1892 at £4 10s in England and £5 10s in America and on the Continent.

The American census caught it as volume. US production to 30 June 1890 was 4,466,926 short tons against 1,145,711 in 1880, a rise of 290 per cent: Bessemer steel from 985,208 to 3,788,572 tons, open-hearth from 84,302 to 504,851. Crucible steel crept from 76,201 to 85,536 tons and stopped, "the high cost of manufacture" preventing it "from successfully competing in price with the other processes mentioned."

## The Basic Lining

Phosphorus still walled off most of the world's ore. In 1870 a Birkbeck tutor, Dr George Chaloner, told his evening class that "the man who eliminates phosphorus by means of the Bessemer converter will make his fortune". In the room sat Sidney Gilchrist Thomas, a Metropolitan Police court clerk who took it as an instruction.

He was not first to the idea. George James Snelus, once works chemist at Dowlais, removed phosphorus in a lime-lined vessel in 1871 and patented it, then could not make a lining that survived the blow. His cousin Percy Carlyle Gilchrist became analytical chemist at Blaenavon in 1876, and from 1877 Thomas came down at weekends at their own expense. Their answer was a lining of dolomite and dead-burned lime instead of silica, plus a longer afterblow: the phosphorus oxidises and stays locked in the basic slag instead of reverting to the metal. The slag sold as fertiliser, "Thomas meal".

Thomas announced the result at the Iron and Steel Institute in March 1878 and patented it in May, meeting open disbelief that an amateur could have solved it. Scaling it fell to Edward Windsor Richards at Bolckow, Vaughan's Eston works near Middlesbrough; the process was finished in 1879. Andrew Carnegie paid some $250,000 for the American rights: the cousins had "struck the useless phosphoric ore and transformed it into steel... a far greater miracle". By 1882 fourteen ironworks across Britain, France, Belgium, Germany, Russia and Austria-Hungary had bought in, and the ores of Lorraine became a strategic asset. Thomas died in Paris on 1 February 1885, aged 34.

## Whose Process Was It

The convenient story is that one man invented it. In Kentucky, William Kelly had been blowing air through molten iron at Eddyville since the early 1850s. He opposed Bessemer's US application, won a priority ruling on the "air boiling" process, and took US patent 17,628 in June 1857. Kelly never made it pay; American licensees bought his patents and sold the combined rights under Bessemer's name. Nor was Sheffield first to run it commercially: G. F. Göransson of Gävle began trials in November 1857 and by October 1858 reported his firm had "entirely given up the manufacture of bar iron".

## Why the Open Hearth Won Anyway

The rival was already running. Carl Wilhelm Siemens's regenerative furnace recycled exhaust heat through brick chequerwork to reach steelmaking temperature on ordinary fuel, and Pierre-Émile Martin licensed it for steel in 1865, under an English patent dated 18 August that year. Bessemer, who had claimed the fusion of steel in a bath of pig iron in his own 1855 patent, noted the gap and then disclaimed priority.

The open hearth heated the bath from outside, so the operator owned the clock. A heat took eight hours or more rather than twenty minutes, which sounds like a defect and was the advantage: there was time to draw a sample, analyse it and correct the charge before tapping. It also ate cold scrap and drove no nitrogen into the steel.

Peter King's reconstruction of British output shows how completely this told. Acid Bessemer rose from 7,676 tons in 1860 to a peak of 1,826,510 in 1889, then fell. By 1897 the two open-hearth routes made 2,809,894 tons against 1,884,155 from both converter routes, while puddled wrought iron slid from 4,732,576 tons in 1875 to 1,288,159.

Bessemer's converter was outlived by a slower machine; he collected royalties "considerably exceeding a million sterling" all the same. The twenty-minute blow had done the thing that mattered: it took steel from £50 a ton to £4 10s, and turned a material sold by the pound into one sold by the shipload.
